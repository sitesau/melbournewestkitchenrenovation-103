The first decision is not the benchtop colour. It is whether the existing footprint stays. A makeover that keeps the current layout will usually sit in a different cost and disruption bracket from a rebuild involving custom joinery, structural changes or moved plumbing and electrical points.

That matters for inner-west weatherboards, period houses and larger growth-corridor homes because a layout change can pull in demolition, rough-in work, cabinetry, wiring and plumbing at the same time. Surface material alone rarely explains the quote.

A useful starting brief is simple: how the kitchen works now, what must change, what can remain, and which problems are layout problems rather than finish problems. For broader context on common misunderstandings, see more on kitchen renovations melbourne west myths.

This applies to owners comparing a refresh, households preparing an older property for sale, and families who need the room to remain usable while decisions are finalised.

Questions for measure-up day

The measure-up is where vague plans become measurable constraints. Every project is described as beginning with a free in-home measure-up and an itemised quote, so use that visit to test assumptions rather than simply collect a number.

  • Which walls, services, vents or storage limits could alter the design?
  • Are there signs that previous work may affect plumbing, electrical or cabinetry fit?
  • Will the drawings shown before manufacture match the version being priced?
  • Which existing elements remain if a lower-cost makeover is chosen?
  • What needs templating, trade coordination or temporary loss of the room?

For an older home, this is often the point where later surprises can be reduced. If access, uneven surfaces or service positions are found early, they can be reflected in scope before production starts rather than negotiated during installation.

Reading the quote line by line

A short lump-sum figure can hide the decisions that move price. Ask for an itemised, fixed-scope written quote that separates demolition, rough-in, cabinetry, benchtops, splashback, plumbing, electrical and appliance installation. Then check whether each line is included, excluded or allowance-based.

The practical test is whether a later variation has a clear reference point. If cabinetry detail, benchtop material or layout changes after the quote, the written breakdown shows what is actually moving. It also reveals whether the cheaper scope is realistic: a makeover only stays cheaper if the elements expected to remain are named and protected.

Confirm the drawings, material selections and inclusions before signing. If an enquiry receives a same-day response aim, treat that as a service expectation rather than proof of fit; the stronger signal is a quote that survives detailed questions.

Timing: where the weeks actually go

The timeline usually turns on fabrication more than demolition. A standard full renovation is commonly 6 to 12 weeks from contract to handover, with cabinetry and benchtop fabrication often taking 4 to 6 weeks and the on-site stage around 2 to 4 weeks for demolition, install, plumbing, electrical and finishing.

The sequence matters. If drawings or material choices are still moving, fabrication can become the slow point. If the design is settled early, the shorter on-site period is easier to plan around, particularly where the kitchen is the main cooking space.

A makeover is generally quicker, but there is no universal duration for every refresh. The safer request is a stage-by-stage timeline covering templating, trade coordination and any period when the room cannot be used. That gives a more reliable answer than a single headline date.

Paperwork, stone ban and handover

For domestic building work over $10,000 in Victoria, a written major domestic building contract is required under the Domestic Building Contracts Act 1995. Before signing, check that scope, price, payment stages and the variations process are clear. The current threshold should be confirmed with Consumer Affairs Victoria and the building regulator rather than assumed.

Projects should run with the right permits and registered plumbing and electrical trades, in line with Victorian building requirements. At completion, the working kitchen should be handed over with warranty and compliance paperwork in writing; that documentation, not benchtop delivery, marks the practical end.

Material choice also needs updating. Engineered stone with 1% or more crystalline silica has been banned for manufacture, supply, processing and installation across Australia since 1 July 2024. Options named as alternatives include porcelain and sintered stone, natural stone, laminate, stainless steel, solid timber and compliant low-silica alternatives. Ask how the surface connects to cabinet layout, edge details and splashback treatment so the specification remains current.
